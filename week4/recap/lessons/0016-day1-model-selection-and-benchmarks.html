Choosing a model & reading benchmarks

"Which LLM is best?" is the wrong question. Here's the right one.

Why this matters for the switch: "which model should we use, and why?" is asked in every AI role. Answering it with a process — basics first, benchmarks second, your own task last — is exactly the judgment that gets an AI engineer hired.

Today's win

You can run the two-phase model-selection workflow and explain why a high benchmark score is "the first indication, not the only indication."

The selection workflow

There is no single "best" model — only the best for this task. The course's process: 1) filter by basics (open vs closed, context window, cost, speed, license, knowledge cutoff), then 2) compare benchmarks to shortlist, then 3) validate on your own problem.

Key concepts

  • Phase 1 — the basics. Parameters, context window, cost, speed, license, knowledge cutoff. These rule models in or out before you ever look at a score.
  • Chinchilla scaling law. Compute-optimal training pairs parameters with data at roughly ~20 training tokens per parameter; doubling params wants ~double the data. (Less central now that compression, RAG, and reasoning let small models punch above their weight.)
  • GPQA — 448 PhD-level science Q&A. PhDs score ~65%, non-PhDs with Google ~34%.
  • MMLU-Pro replaced the saturated, ambiguous MMLU: 10 options instead of 4, harder questions.
  • HLE (Humanity's Last Exam) — ~2,500 deliberately superhuman questions; models scored ~2–3% at release. Other names: AMC (math), LiveCodeBench (rotating coding problems), MUSE (reasoning).
  • Benchmarks have fatal flaws: data contamination (test questions leak into training), overfitting to a single metric, narrow scope, and saturation. Apple's experiment — changing facts but not intent — tanked scores, proving memorization.
  • Reasoning amplifies results. Forcing a model to explain before answering (e.g. the Connect Four leaderboard) dramatically improves it — direct prediction fails.
Interview-ready framing: "Why not just pick the top of the leaderboard?" — Because of contamination and overfitting: a model tuned to ace GPQA may collapse when the questions are slightly altered. Benchmarks start your shortlist; your own eval ends it.
